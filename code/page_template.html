    Between 1574 and 1579 Christopher Saxton surveyed the counties of England and Wales, had them engraved and published them in 1579 as the first atlas of its counties. How good was it? I read {{counts.readings|int}} towns, bridges and river mouths on all {{counts.sheets_county_total|int}} county maps and on the general map, and set each against where the place really is.

    01Saxton's towns are where they should be

    I fitted each sheet to the real ground with one scale, one turn and one shift, so that every sheet is judged on its own terms. Across {{counts.used_county|int}} readings the median town sits {{county.med_km|f1}} km from where its sheet puts it; {{county.within1_pct|pc}} are within 1 km and all but {{county.over5|int}} within 5 km. The best drawn sheet is {{county.best_name}} (median {{county.best_km|f2}} km), the weakest {{county.worst_name}} ({{county.worst_km|f2}} km). Allowing each sheet to stretch (an affine fit) only brings the median to {{county.med_aff_km|f1}} km, so the shapes are true and not squashed: the median sheet is stretched by a factor of only {{scale.aniso_median|f2}}.

    02North leans to the west

    The surprise is the direction. On {{turn.n_pos|int}} of {{turn.n|int}} county sheets, true north is turned anticlockwise: the top of the sheet points east of true north. The middle half of sheets lean between {{turn.q1_deg|f1}}° and {{turn.q3_deg|f1}}°, with a median of {{turn.median_deg|f1}}° (Cornwall excluded). That is close to the compass variation that William Borough measured at Limehouse in 1580, about 11¼° east, which is what you would expect if the surveys were laid out by magnetic compass. The general map of 1579, by contrast, is turned only {{turn.general_deg|f1}}°. Cornwall is the outlier: turned {{turn.cornwall_deg|f0}}° the other way, possibly so that the long peninsula fits the plate.

    03The general map against the Gough Map

    On the general map of England and Wales ({{general.n|int}} towns read) the median town is {{general.med_km|f1}} km out, at a scale about {{scale.county_over_general_median|f1}} times smaller than a typical county sheet. The Gough Map, about two centuries older and measured the same way, misplaces its median way station by {{gough.med_km|f1}} km, {{gough.ratio_general|f1}} times as much.

    04Sheets agree with each other

    {{shared.n|int}} towns appear on two neighbouring county sheets. Taking each back to the ground through its own sheet's fit, the two positions differ by a median of {{shared.med_km|f2}} km, the most being {{shared.max_name}} ({{shared.max_km|f1}} km). Scales differ a lot: {{scale.max_sheet}} is drawn {{scale.ratio|f1}} times larger than {{scale.min_sheet}}.

    05How I did this

    The scans are the Bibliothèque nationale de France copy of the atlas on Wikimedia Commons (41 files: the general map, the 34 county maps, a map of Scotland bound with them and five single sheets from other copies, which duplicate Devon, Dorset, Cornwall, Wiltshire and Somerset and were not used). For each sheet I read town symbols (church or castle), named in the engraving, on zoomed crops, guided by a prediction from the towns already read, and matched each to its Wikidata settlement point; I flagged each reading as certain, probable or doubtful in its exact point. {{counts.conf1_pct|pc}} of county readings are certain. True positions were put on a flat frame in kilometres centred on each sheet. Every reading was then checked again on a zoomed crop; misplaced symbols were corrected and doubtful identifications dropped. A thin plate spline bends the true grid onto each sheet.

    Caveats
    • I read all {{counts.sheets_county_total|int}} county sheets and the general map.
    • Wikidata points mark a modern centre, often a few hundred metres from the old church, so part of each misplacement is mine, not Saxton's.
    • Readings by eye on 3,840 px scans; the scanner's own skew may add a degree to the turns.
    • The magnetic explanation of the turn is a reading of the pattern, not proof; I did not test the scale bars.
